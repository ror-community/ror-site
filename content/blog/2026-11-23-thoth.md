[Thoth Open Metadata](https://thoth.pub), a community-led, non-profit metadata platform for open access books and chapters, explains why it built its data model around ROR, how ROR IDs travel from Thoth into Crossref, ONIX and open APIs, and why open organisation identifiers matter for the long tail of book publishing.
{{< /callout >}}

{{< figure src="/img/blog/thoth/THOTH_ColourPos.png" class="featured-figure" alt="Thots logo." >}}

Thoth Open Metadata is a UK-registered, non-profit Community Interest Company that emerged from the work conducted within the [Copim community](https://copim.pub/)’s two Arcadia and Research England/UKRI-funded projects (COPIM and Open Book Futures). We provide a free, open source platform through which small-to-medium-sized independent, scholar-led, and institutional university and library presses create, manage and disseminate metadata for their open access books and chapters. Today, more than 110 publishers worldwide use Thoth, and our database holds records for close to 6,300 books and more than 21,000 chapters – all of them released under a CC0 public domain dedication.

The reason we needed ROR is a problem that anyone working in book publishing will recognise. Long-form scholarship travels through a highly fragmented supply chain, and a book’s data points are being communicated through a variety of metadata formats, including multiple variants of ONIX for retail and aggregators, MARC for library catalogues, KBART for knowledge bases, and XML for DOI registration (to name but a few). Each of these hand-offs is an opportunity for information to get lost – a phenomenon we have taken to calling the "leaky metadata pipeline." Affiliations entered as free text are among the first casualties: "Univ. of Sussex," "Sussex University" and "University of Sussex, Faculty of History" are, to a machine, three different things. As a result, books and chapters remain notoriously under-represented in research information systems, institutional reporting and funder monitoring, which in turn makes it harder for the very institutions that support book authors and contributors to edited collections to see, and value, that work.

When we started designing Thoth's data model in 2020, it was therefore clear that we needed an open, persistent identifier for organisations. When my colleague Hannah Hillen and I later summarised the takeaways of the OABN and OIPA webinar on [good metadata practice for open access books](https://doi.org/10.21428/785a6451.ced96be1) in 2025, one of them was precisely that good OA book metadata should identify the entities associated with a work without ambiguity, using persistent identifiers wherever possible. For institutional data, ROR was the obvious fit, and for reasons that go beyond its technical merits:


* **It is open in the same way Thoth is open.** ROR data is released under a CC0 dedication, exactly like the metadata that publishers make available via the Thoth platform and database. Building on a proprietary or licence-restricted organisation identifier would [have undermined](https://doi.org/10.21428/785a6451.eb0d86e8) the promise we make to publishers, libraries and the wider ecosystem that every record coming out of Thoth can be reused without restrictions.
* **It is curated, and therefore unique.** Persistence is only half of what we need from an identifier; uniqueness is the other half. In “[Maybe Persistent but Certainly Not Unique,](https://doi.org/10.21428/785a6451.fb6181fd)," we looked at how a single open access book can end up with four, five or more DOIs minted by different platforms, and contrasted this with self-managed identifiers such as ORCID, and ROR's curated, multilingual registry on the other. We believe ROR’s community curation model is exactly what makes ROR reliable. The same blog post also showed what happens without it: when major book platforms keep their own proprietary institution lists, institutional usage data becomes very hard to correlate across services.
* **It is community-governed infrastructure.** ROR is run by and for the academic community, which matters to us as an organisation that has assessed itself against the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI). As part of our [POSI self-assessment](https://doi.org/10.70950/sllm9969), we outline our commitment to interoperability and bibliodiversity by designing our services so that they complement and strengthen other open infrastructures rather than replacing or enclosing them. Establishing our own authority list of institutions would have been counter-intuitive, and exactly the kind of duplication we want to avoid.
* **One identifier for affiliations and funders.** Many of the organisations our authors are affiliated with also fund their books. ROR allowed us to treat these as one and the same entity, a decision that has only been reinforced by Crossref's move to accept ROR IDs wherever Funder IDs were previously used.

This thinking has since been written into the [International Metadata Recommendations and Platform-Specific Requirements for Open Access Books and Chapters](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18173982) report that we published together with international metadata and library experts in January 2026. The report flags DOI, ORCID and ROR as *the* persistent identifiers deemed essential for tracking, attribution and interoperability, while also noting that adoption so far has been uneven, particularly in the Humanities and Social Sciences and across the wide archipelago of small-to-medium-sized presses. In its two-tiered metadata framework, ROR IDs for contributor affiliations and for funders sit in the "Desirable" tier, and publishers are encouraged to identify themselves via ROR as part of the "Essential" tier. Our aim with Thoth is simple: to make doing the "desirable" thing the easiest thing for a small press to do.


### How Thoth uses ROR**2) How do you use ROR?**

ROR sits at the heart of how Thoth describes organisations. Rather than keeping separate lists of "funders" and "affiliations," Thoth’s data model has a single **institution** entity, and each institution record carries its name, country, ROR ID and, where one exists, its legacy Crossref Funder Registry DOI. That one record is then linked to works in two ways:


* **Affiliations** connect a contributor's role on a specific book or chapter to an institution, so that a scholar who has moved institutions can be attributed correctly for each piece of work. Because chapters are first-class records in Thoth, this works at chapter level too, which matters a great deal for edited collections with contributors from many different institutions: with more than 19,000 of all affiliations in Thoth, over 70%, are recorded on individual chapters.
* **Funding** data connects a work to the institution that supported it, alongside programme, project name and grant number.

In practice, the vast majority of these links resolve to ROR. As of 28 October 2026, Thoth's public API shows:

These percentages describe the affiliations publishers have recorded, not how many records carry an affiliation in the first place. F… for the overall count, the picture is more mixed. Of the more than 46,000 contribution records in Thoth (meaning a person’s role on a given book or chapter), 54% have at least one affiliation, meaning that 46% remain without dedicated affiliation data. At the level of works, 39% of books and 57% of chapters have at least one affiliated contributor. 

Parts of that gap are expected, given the role that independent scholars, translators and practitioners play in academia, and particularly in the Humanities and Social Sciences, where most of the research in Thoth’s dataset is at home. The gap also reflects missing affiliations that were simply never captured, often for backlist titles, reflecting a more general issue that had been outlined e.g. by [Dean et al. in 2025](https://doi.org/10.7269/C1CC7B), who note that “affiliation metadata suffers from wide scale gaps and inaccuracies, particularly when it comes to the assignment of persistent identifiers, such as ROR IDs.” With Thoth, we have an open tool in place to help tackle that issue, and once an affiliation is recorded in Thoth, it is almost always tied to a ROR ID; the bigger task ahead of us, still, is getting it recorded at all.

It is also worth noting the interconnected nature of PIDs. What makes all of this valuable is not the identifier sitting in our database, but where it goes next. As I have highlighted in a recent [Voices of OPERAS UK interview with Jisc](https://research.jiscinvolve.org/wp/2026/09/25/voices-of-operas-uk-thoth-open-metadata/), Thoth sees itself as a highly connective infrastructure rather than a destination platform, and persistent identifiers such as ROR are a large part of what makes those connections hold. Thoth carries ROR IDs into the outputs publishers rely on:

* **Crossref DOI deposits**: book and chapter deposits generated by Thoth include contributor affiliations with the ROR ID as institution identifier, so they flow into Crossref's open metadata and on to everyone who builds on it.
* **ONIX 3.0 and 3.1**: Thoth's full ONIX 3.0 and 3.1 records identify funders with their ROR ID and, where one exists, their legacy Funder Registry DOI, so that recipients who have not yet moved to ROR are not left behind. Our ONIX 3.1 records additionally carry ROR IDs for contributor affiliations (affiliation identifier type 40, ROR).
* **Open APIs and harvesting**: ROR IDs are available via Thoth's GraphQL API and the Export API's JSON output, released under CC0.

Next to the obvious benefits for discovery and attribution, this opens up some very concrete use cases for the open access books community. Take, for example, the [Open Book Collective](https://openbookcollective.org/), our sister organisation from the Copim community, through which libraries collectively fund open access book publishers and infrastructures. Thoth provides the metadata infrastructure behind the OBC's [Collective Catalogue](https://catalogue.openbookcollective.org/books), which currently holds rich data of close to 2,400 titles across 12 OBC publishers. With reliable, ROR-based affiliation data flowing into that catalogue, it becomes possible for the OBC Outreach team to show a library exactly which openly available books and chapters its own researchers have authored or edited. Likewise, funders can find the books they have supported, and institutions can include long-form outputs in their research information systems without the tedious work of disambiguating name strings by hand. All of this is what sector-wide international initiatives such as the [Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information](https://barcelona-declaration.org/) are calling for – and books have every right to be part of it!


### Lessons from Thoth's ROR integration**3) What were the steps you took to integrate ROR into your systems and workflows?**

As Thoth's [open source codebase](https://github.com/thoth-pub/thoth) and its public changelog document, our ROR integration has grown in a phased, iterative fashion, alongside the rest of the platform:


1. **Early data model (2020–2021).** The first versions of Thoth kept a separate table of funders, identified by Funder Registry DOIs, while contributor affiliations were recorded as free text. ROR first arrived on funder records in summer 2021. 
2. **One institution table, built around ROR (November 2021).** With Thoth v0.6.0, we replaced the funder table with a single institution table and standardised contributor affiliations against it, so that every organisation, whether affiliation or funder, could carry a ROR ID. Existing records were migrated to the new structure.
3. **Editing and ordering affiliations (May 2022).** Publishers gained the ability to edit contributions and their affiliations directly in the Thoth interface, selecting institutions from the ROR-backed institution list rather than typing names.
4. **ROR in Crossref deposits (October 2022).** Thoth v0.9.0 began outputting affiliations (with ROR) and funding information in its Crossref DOI deposit files, alongside references and abstracts.
5. **ROR in comprehensive ONIX (2024–2025).** Our full 'Thoth' ONIX 3.0 (March 2024) and ONIX 3.1 (March 2025) specifications expose ROR IDs for funders, with ONIX 3.1 adding them for contributor affiliations too, giving downstream platforms a standards-based route to the same information.
6. **Thoth 1.0 (April 2026).** Our major 1.0 release introduced multilingual metadata, accessibility metadata, direct content upload, and bulk back catalogue ingest. A dedicated OAI-PMH endpoint is still in the works, and the `oai-openaire` flavour will carry ROR IDs for affiliations and funders, meaning ROR-linked records will reach an even wider range of harvesters.

A few lessons from that journey may be useful for others in the book space:

**Make the identifier the path of least resistance.** Publishers rarely add a PID because a guideline tells them to; they do it because the web interface offers the right organisation as they type. At a [publishers' workshop](https://copim.pubpub.org/pub/publishers-workshop-2024-accessibility-archiving-open-metadata/release/1) held in November 2024, presses from six countries flagged the labour-intensive nature of manual data entry, particularly having to juggle several information sources (such as the ROR registry and the Thema subject classifications dictionary) on their screens at once, among their main pain points. Pairing a pick-list with ROR's curated organisation names within Thoth’s interface has done more for data quality than any amount of documentation.

**Keep the legacy identifier while the ecosystem catches up.** Several platforms in the book supply chain still expect Funder Registry DOIs. Holding both identifiers on the same record lets us serve them today without blocking the move to ROR tomorrow.

**Not every contributor is at home at an institution.** Independent scholars, translators, artists and practitioners are a normal part of the publishing process in the Humanities and Social Sciences – a strong argument why affiliation attribution should remain optional. The roughly 2.5% of affiliations without a ROR ID largely reflect organisations that are not (yet) in the registry, such as small research centres, collectives and the publishers themselves.

**Check what survives downstream.** Sending a ROR ID from the upstream source does not mean it is being retained downstream at the variety of aggregator and index platforms active in the wider scholarly communications ecosystem. We have learned to inspect what aggregators and discovery systems actually keep, and to feed what we find back into our export templates.

Now, if you are a publisher looking for an easy way to implement ROR IDs together with the wider set of metadata, everything described above is already available to publishers free of charge: [Thoth Oasis](https://thoth.pub/packages/oasis), our free, self-service package, gives any open access book publisher the same metadata management tools our dissemination clients use. Publishers can create and manage records for books and chapters, and export them in the various formats the supply chain expects: ONIX3, MARC, KBART, CSV, BibTeX and Crossref XML. All of this runs on open APIs, with no platform lock-in. Oasis is our commitment that open metadata provision should never depend on costly subscriptions to third-party providers.

This particularly matters for PIDs: when a publisher adds a contributor in Thoth, it can attach that person’s ORCID iD. Affiliations and funders are picked from Thoth’s institution list, more than 99% of which carry a ROR ID, rather than a free-text entry. The interface then shows the ROR icon next to each affiliation, so the editor can check at a glance that the right organisation has been recorded. Since the release of Thoth 1.0 in April 2026, the Thoth platform additionally encourages publishers to contribute to the ROR community by inviting them to add (or update) institutions they cannot directly identify within the ROR database via ROR’s [curation request](https://ror.org/registry) workflow. 

Starting from that single data entry point, every export Thoth generates for that particular title carries the identifier with it, without any further work required from the publisher. For a publisher that registers its own DOIs with Crossref, the readily-available Crossref XML export already includes ROR-identified affiliations, ready to deposit.

There are several ways to get started. Following an initial registration and setup of a publisher’s account in Thoth, presses can directly start creating new records within the Thoth interface, bulk-upload existing back catalogue metadata as ONIX 3.0 or CSV files, or connect their Open Monograph Press installation through the [OMP–Thoth plugin](https://github.com/thoth-pub/thoth-omp-plugin). The Thoth team is happy to support whichever route suits them. Presses that later want automated DOI registration, distribution to more than fifteen aggregator and index platforms, automated archiving, usage statistics and/or a full catalogue and website can move to our [paid packages](https://thoth.pub/pricing)  whenever they like. The metadata stays the same, open and high-quality record it has been from the start.

Next to the guidance already implemented within the platform in the form of tooltips and indicators that highlight which metadata fields might be good to include to match requirements of a variety of aggregator platforms and indexes, we also have the[ International Metadata Recommendations report](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18173982) and the slide deck of our recent[ OBC webinar on open metadata workflows with Thoth](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23348811) as resources that can walk publishers through what good practice looks like for books and chapters, field by field. If you are a publisher wondering where to begin, please do not hesitate to [get in touch](mailto:info@thoth.pub).

There is also work still ahead of us, and we are happy to be open about it. Our Crossref deposits currently still identify funders via Funder Registry DOIs, and in line with our role as a recently-[accredited](https://doi.org/10.70950/jier8451) Crossref Advanced Service Provider, we plan to further improve those deposits by moving the funding data over to ROR now that Crossref accepts ROR IDs for funders. We would also like to add a ROR ID to Thoth's [publisher records](https://thoth.pub/publishers), so that presses themselves can be identified unambiguously, and to explore ROR integration in further outputs such as our MARC records.


### **4) What else would you like to say about ROR? Final thoughts and next steps**

First of all, a heartfelt thank you to the ROR team and its curators. A registry like this only works because of patient, often invisible work on names, aliases, relationships and new records … and for a small non-profit infrastructure like Thoth, being able to rely on that work, rather than having to replicate it in-house, makes a real difference.

Secondly, we would like to invite the book community, and particularly the small presses we work with, to engage with ROR more actively, both by requesting records and by actually using them. Curious about this, we had a quick peek at two recent Zenodo records our team has been involved in. In the [workshop materials on collective funding for Diamond OA](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23033528) that the Open Book Collective deposited this September, both the Open Book Collective (ror.org/043be3q74) and Thoth (ror.org/00dyx6f45) are identified via ROR. In the Zenodo record of our own [metadata recommendations report](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18173982), however, Thoth appears without its ROR ID, as do Open Book Publishers and punctum books. The registry was not the problem: the identifier simply was not picked up at the point of entry. If even a metadata infrastructure can slip up here, it shows how much depends on the tools and workflows in front of the person depositing. Where an organisation genuinely is not yet in the registry, anyone can request a new record or a correction through [ROR's curation process](https://ror.org/registry/), and every request helps the next press, repository or funder that needs to identify that organisation.

Thirdly, organisation identifiers matter for bibliodiversity. Open access books are published in dozens of languages, by institutions across Latin America, Africa, Asia and Europe whose names do not always translate neatly into English. Recording organisations by identifier rather than by English-language string is one of the most practical ways we know of making non-English scholarship visible in global research information systems, and we are very glad to be sharing this post in Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese as well as English.

Finally, the bigger picture. Open access books have long lagged behind journals in policy, infrastructure and metadata maturity, and the gap is most visible precisely in the connections between works, people and organisations. Every ROR ID that travels intact from a small press, via Thoth, into Crossref, a library catalogue or a funder's monitoring dashboard narrows that gap a little. In the words of our [POSI self-assessment](https://doi.org/10.70950/sllm9969), "open by default" is not enough: openness has to be matched by shared, community-governed infrastructure that others can build on, and by funding models that keep it in community hands. 

All in all, we are proud to be part of the community of organisations building on ROR, and we are looking forward to the next steps on our roadmap together! If you are a publisher, librarian or funder interested in how ROR, and Thoth, can help make open access books more visible, please do not hesitate to [get in touch](mailto:info@thoth.pub).
